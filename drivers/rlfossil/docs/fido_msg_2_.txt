However, you should be able to limit the datagram size at your end.
Every piece of TCP/IP s/w (from Trumpet for Windoze to DOS based NOS
implementations for packet radio) I've seen allow you to limit your MTU
(IP datagram size) and MSS (TCP Maximum segment) to suit your link.
I've always used smallish datagrams for my links (216b for packet to fit
in a single AX.25 frame) and 512k for my Windoze based PPP connection.
This may or may not help you (my TCP/IP theory is a little rusty ;-) ).

 ro> For those unfamiliar with the explanation above, a brief tutorial.
 ro> When you send a block of data via the internet, it's known as a
 ro> "datagram". The links that the datagram goes through may not be able
 ro> to pass the entire datagram in one piece, so the IP protocol fragments
 ro> it into smaller pieces. At the remote end, the datagram is
 ro> reassembled, and then passed to the application in its entirety as a
 ro> single block.

As I remember, datagram size is negotiated over the whole link.  Try
setting a smaller datagram size (MTU and MSS) at your end, and see if
things improve.  For example, on packet radio, we used a MTU of 216
bytes, so the datagram plus 40 byte IP overhead could fit within a
single AX.25 (packet link layer) frame, and avoid this fragmentation
problem.  However, in that case, the network was homogenous (except a
few people running Ethernet LANS at their station, and the AXIP
'wormholes', again Ethernet links between the packet gateway and the
Internet).

 ro> And there lays the potential problem . . . if someone is sending you a
 ro> large datagram then your application may not be given any data for a
 ro> relatively long period of time. For an application such as FrontDoor
 ro> which normally expects to be connected via modem directly to the
 ro> remote system, it's not unreasonable for it to start getting concerned
 ro> when no data is presented to it for a period of time (although IMHO
 ro> it's a wee bit *too* impatient in that regard).

What size are you using for your MTU and MSS?  Try 512 bytes or smaller
if you can change them.
